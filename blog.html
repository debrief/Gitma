A trained manoeuvre detector: no better than the classical one

November 19, 2026 · 2 min read

Three of our designs stumbled on the same question: has the target just turned, or is the bearing error playing tricks? So we trained a network for exactly that question, on data with the realistic bearing error, and compared it with the best classical change detector (a CUSUM on a tracker's innovations) tuned to the same error.

What we found​

  • Every detector is weak when false alarms must be rare. Allowing one false alarm every two hours on quiet stretches, the best CUSUM spots only 12% of turns within 5 minutes. Under clean, uncorrelated error the figure was 28%.
  • The trained network did not beat it: 11% within 5 minutes, and slower on the turns both caught. It was no better than simply watching the tracking network's own course estimate (11%).
  • With more false alarms allowed the learned signals edge ahead. At two false alarms an hour they catch about 35% against the CUSUM's 33%. That is not the operating point we committed to.

What it means​

Correlated bearing error makes quiet legs look like gentle turns, and that, not the choice of detector, is the limit. This is why the composite solver and adaptive process noise failed, and a trained detector does not remove it. Training choices (rewarding early alarms rather than sustained ones, for instance) could still help, but nothing here suggests a large gain.

Fading the network on straight legs: the trade-off holds

November 18, 2026 · 2 min read

The EKF fed with the network's range passed its gate overall, but on a target's straight leg it was worse than the plain EKF. We tried two fixes together: let the network's weight fade once the bearings fix range, and let the filter's process noise rise only when the bearings look surprising (after a manoeuvre). A setting had to stay ahead overall and come within 10% of the EKF on straight legs. None did, so the phase stopped before using a fresh test set.

What we found​

  • Adapting the process noise just moves the trade-off. If it reacts readily, overall course recovers but straight-leg course gets worse; if it reacts rarely, the reverse. Under correlated bearing error, "surprising bearings" do not reliably mean a manoeuvre.
  • The fade does what it says, at a price. It brings straight-leg course from 4.0° to 3.6° (the EKF manages 3.1°) but gives up the network's range overall: 13.9% error instead of 12.5%.
  • Even with the reference's own process noise, adding the network costs about 20% on straight-leg course. Its range errors are correlated in time and leak into the velocity estimate.

What it means​

Straight legs belong to the classical methods (the EKF, and least squares even more so); the network's range helps after manoeuvres and overall. The phase-15 tracker stays the best compromise found. Splitting the work explicitly, a classical solution on straight legs and the fused tracker after manoeuvres, is what the composite solver tried earlier; its weak point was detecting the manoeuvre, the same weakness seen here.

The network's range inside an EKF beats the range-tuned EKF (with caveats)

November 16, 2026 · 2 min read

Re-tuning the classical trackers to realistic bearing error stopped the IMM-based fused tracker: after a target manoeuvre it held course worse than a plain EKF. So we fed the network's range estimate into an EKF instead, tuned its process noise together with the fusion weights on validation data, and judged the one chosen setting once on 40,000 fresh scenarios.

What we found​

  • It passed G5 against the re-tuned EKF. Range error 12.8% against 16.3% (0.78 ×) and course error 28.9° against 35.3° (0.82 ×) over all gate rows; on settled rows (pooled before and after manoeuvres), significantly worse on course than the EKF (1.04 ×, interval 1.01–1.08) but within the allowed 10% margin. We had forecast only a 10% chance of a pass.
  • The network is needed, but it is not the whole story. The same EKF with the higher process noise and no network fails the gate (a check run after scoring, reported only). About half the course gain comes from that process noise alone, which the reference, tuned for range, never used.
  • Straight legs still belong to the classical methods. Before any manoeuvre the plain EKF holds course to 3.3° and least squares to 2.2°; the fused tracker manages 5.2°. It wins after a manoeuvre (22° against 33°). The gate pools both, and the mix decides it.
  • It does not beat everything. Against the re-tuned IMM, course is level (0.99 ×). The network alone still has the lowest overall range (12.0%) and course (26.0°).

What it means​

The project's qualified go gets firmer: a learned range aid inside a classical tracker beats the EKF re-tuned (for range) to the same error, on range and overall course, though not the re-tuned IMM on course. It remains a range aid. On a long straight leg the network's correlated range error makes the tracker worse than bearings alone, so a weight that fades once the bearings fix range is the obvious next step.

Re-arming the composite solver makes it worse

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read

The first composite solver almost passed: it missed its gate by 1.3% on course. Its weakness seemed clear. Once it handed over to the fused tracker after a manoeuvre, it never came back to least squares, even when the target settled on a new straight leg.

The owner chose to let it re-arm:

  • after a switch, fit a new straight leg, borrowing the fused tracker's range to pin it down;
  • hand back to that leg once it is a few minutes long and fits the bearings well.

Result: worse, not better​

  • The score. The best re-armed setting scores −0.031, against −0.013 for the original. It fails overall course against the fused tracker (1.034).
  • The pattern. Every setting does worse than without re-arming, and handing back sooner does worst.

Why​

  • The new legs look right but aren't. Where the composite uses one, it is about twice as wrong as the fused tracker on the same rows: range 31% against 16%, speed 5.9 kt against 3.0 kt.
  • A good fit isn't a right answer. Without the listening platform manoeuvring again, many different tracks explain a short leg's bearings equally well. Borrowing the range fixes the distance but not the velocity. This is the basic limit of bearings-only tracking, and it is why least squares needs an ownship manoeuvre in the first place.
  • The real cost is unchanged. 44% of the rows after a manoeuvre still come from the stale least-squares answer before the switch trips. Re-arming does nothing for those.

What it means​

Both composite designs are recorded as failing their gate on validation. No test data were generated. The lesson is narrow: a residual check can say when a straight-line fit has broken, but not when a new one is trustworthy.

A composite solver: the best of both, nearly — stopped at validation

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read

Spec 024 found that batch least squares reaches the information limit while the target sails straight, and that the fused tracker is best once the target has manoeuvred. The obvious next step was a composite:

  • report least squares while its straight-line fit still explains the bearings;
  • hand over to the fused tracker once the fit breaks.

The test​

  • The switch: a residual check over the last W bearings against a threshold c. Once tripped, it latches. Nothing is trained.
  • The gate (G9): better than the fused tracker overall on range and course, and within 1.1 × of least squares on straight legs, under realistic bearing error.
  • Pre-registration. W and c are picked on validation by a rule written down first. The composite goes to a fresh test set only if every check clears on validation.

Result: no setting clears all five checks​

The best setting (a 1-minute window, threshold 8) misses by 1.3% on overall course:

Composite againstRangeCourseSpeed
The fused tracker, all rows0.9641.0130.85 (reported)
Least squares, straight legs (limit 1.1)1.021.071.04

Why​

  • On straight legs it works. It keeps least squares' accuracy, with course errors 4 × smaller than the fused tracker's.
  • After a manoeuvre it pays for being late. Until the residual check trips, it keeps reporting least squares' out-of-date straight-line answer. Course there is 12% worse than the fused tracker's.
  • Most of the rows that count come after a manoeuvre, so the late switch outweighs the straight-leg gain on course.
  • Tuning the threshold only trades one failure for the other.
    • Switch earlier, and false alarms from the correlated error throw away least squares on straight legs.
    • Switch later, and the stale answers last longer.

This matches phase 5: detecting a manoeuvre from bearings alone is slow, whatever the detector.

What it means​

The composite is better than the fused tracker on range (about 4%) and clearly better on speed (about 15%). It is not better on course, and the pre-registered rule asked for all three. No test data were generated, and earlier results stand.

Possible next steps, each needing a new decision:

  • a gate that judges straight legs and post-manoeuvre rows separately;
  • a switch that can return to least squares on a new straight leg.

The project in plain English: can AI track a ship from bearings alone?

November 15, 2026 · 3 min read

A non-technical summary of what this project set out to do, what it found, and what it means. The full, technical answer is the go/no-go report.

The question​

A submarine listening to a ship hears only which direction the sound comes from, not how far away the ship is. Working out the ship's range, course and speed from those directions alone is a classic problem called target motion analysis. Well-established mathematical methods already exist for it.

We asked whether an AI model, trained on large amounts of computer-simulated data, could do the job well enough to be useful.

How we worked​

Every step was set up as a fair test:

  • the rules were written down before the test ran;
  • the score came from fresh data the model had never seen;
  • the AI was always compared with the existing mathematical methods.

Each result was then checked by a panel of AI reviewers.

What we found​

  • The AI does learn something real. It clearly beats guesswork, and it is good at judging how far away the ship is.
  • On its own, it isn't good enough. When the ship sails straight, the traditional methods are 1.6 to 3.5 times more accurate, especially on speed. The AI never caught up, despite many attempts.
  • It shines where the traditional methods struggle. That is in the minutes just after the ship turns, and when the direction measurements carry realistic, drifting errors.
  • The best result combines the two. Feeding the AI's range estimate into a traditional tracker made it 18–23% more accurate on range than the tracker alone.
  • A final check measured the theoretical best possible accuracy.
    • On straight legs, the traditional method already reaches that limit, and the AI is about 1.5 times worse.
    • After the ship turns, it is still unknown how much better anything could do.

The verdict​

  • No: the AI should not replace the traditional methods.
  • A qualified yes: it is worth using as a helper inside a traditional tracker, mainly for judging range and for coping after the ship turns.

What would come next​

The next step is testing on real or more realistic data, against a traditional tracker tuned for those conditions. That cannot happen here, because this project is public and uses simulated data only.

Honest caveats​

  • Everything is simulated.
  • Some rules were relaxed along the way. That always happened before the affected result was known, but it still matters.
  • The reviewers were not fully independent. They were AI agents of the same family as the one doing the work.

None of these changes the main "no".

Re-tuning the classical trackers: the fused tracker's course falls short

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read

The severity sweep showed that the classical trackers we compared against had been tuned on white noise, which flattered the fused tracker. So we re-tuned both of them, the IMM and the EKF, to the realistic bearing error, then rebuilt the fused tracker on the re-tuned IMM and tried its 24 fusion settings again. To go to a gate, a setting had to beat both re-tuned trackers on G5's five checks.

What we found​

  • Re-tuning helps the classical trackers a little. Range error falls from 16.1% to 15.8% for the EKF and from 16.7% to 15.1% for the IMM. Both do it by treating the correlated error as heavier white noise.
  • The fused tracker's range advantage survives. Its best setting has 12.5% range error: 0.79 × the EKF's and 0.83 × the IMM's.
  • Its course after a manoeuvre does not. Once the target has settled, the re-tuned EKF holds course to 8.3°; the best fused tracker manages 10.7°, 1.29 × the EKF and over the 1.1 limit. No setting comes close enough, so the phase stopped before the gate, as forecast.
  • Against the re-tuned IMM alone it would have passed validation. Choosing the harder of the two references decided the outcome.

What it means​

The network improves range; the IMM it feeds still sets the course, and a plain EKF does that better on settled legs. The fused tracker is a range aid, not a better tracker overall. One condition of the project's qualified go, a fair re-tuned reference, is not met; the owner decides what follows. Fusing the network's range into the EKF instead is the obvious untried design.

How the trackers cope as bearing error gets worse

November 15, 2026 · 2 min read

The project's "qualified go" for the fused tracker rested on one level of realistic bearing error. We re-ran every frozen estimator on the same scenarios at four levels: no correlated error (×0), half (×0.5), the usual level (×1) and double (×2).

What we found​

  • The fused tracker keeps its edge over the classical IMM at every level: its range error is 0.84, 0.79, 0.78 and 0.77 × the IMM's. The edge grows a little, then levels off.
  • Batch least squares stays at the information limit on straight legs at every level. Nothing can do better there.
  • The network on its own overtakes the fused tracker as error grows. At ×0 the fused tracker is better; at ×0.5 they tie; at ×2 the network alone has 20% less range error. The fused tracker inherits the IMM's weakness under heavy error, and its settings were chosen on white noise.
  • The composite solver's switch breaks under heavy error. Correlated error looks like a manoeuvre to its fit check, so at ×2 it gives up least squares on straight legs.

What it means​

The qualified go survives. Two conditions get sharper: the fusion's settings, and the classical reference it is judged against, both need tuning to the error level of real data. Part of the network's robustness is a learned prior about typical geometry, which only helps while real engagements look like the simulated ones.

Phase 11: retraining T2 on realistic bearing error does not improve its range

November 14, 2026 · 3 min read

Phases 7–10 showed that under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), the learned network T2 gives the most robust range estimate. T2 had been trained only on white noise. The obvious next question: does training it on the realistic error make it better still?

The test​

  • The model. T2's architecture and configuration, unchanged, trained from scratch on a fresh 100,000-scenario dataset generated entirely with realistic error. Training took about 16 hours on GitHub Actions and stopped early at epoch 48.
  • The gate (G8). The retrained T2 must:
    • have a median range error wholly below the frozen T2's;
    • pass G5 against the reference IMM.
  • The validation step first. The six point estimates on the shared validation split must all be within their limits before any test data are generated.

Result: no gain on range, so it stops at validation​

The frozen T21.021 [0.991, 1.052]0.9810.878

  • Range is unchanged. 12.4% against the frozen T2's 12.2%, and the interval also rules out a gain of more than 1%.
  • Settled course improves by about 12%, mostly on straight legs before any manoeuvre.
  • It still beats the IMM on overall range and course, and passes G5's settled limits.

The cost: worse on white noise​

On the same scenarios with white noise only, the retrained T2 is clearly worse:

The retrained T2 looks more robust (it degrades 1.21 × against 1.52 ×), but only because it starts worse. On white noise it loses to the IMM on range.

Baselines (realistic error, all gated rows)​

  • Batch least squares: 17.1% and 46.0°. It is best before any manoeuvre (6.9%, 2.0°).
  • EKF: 16.1% and 29.7°. It is best on settled course (9.8°) and speed (1.91 kt).
  • Naive: 38.7% and 88.3°.
  • Fused tracker and hybrid: rebuilt on the retrained T2, they are unchanged (within 0.3 points in range).

What it means​

Seeing the error in training did not buy range accuracy. A plausible reading is that the frozen T2 is already near what the bearings allow under this error model: phase 9 found the drifting bias nearly unobservable, and a network cannot learn what the data do not contain.

Limits:

  • one training run, so the run-to-run spread is unknown;
  • one error model at one severity;
  • validation data only;
  • an architecture that was not re-tuned.

No test data were generated. Phase 10's verdict stands, and the owner decides what comes next.

Phase 10: T2's range with a filter's velocity passes G8, but adds little that was not known

November 12, 2026 · 4 min read

Phases 7–9 left one clear lesson. Under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), the learned network T2 gives the most robust range, and no change to the filters closes the gap. Phase 10 takes the direct route and reports T2's range alongside the fused tracker's course and speed.

The test​

  • The estimator:

    • range and position from T2;
    • course and speed from the frozen fused tracker;
    • T2's whole row wherever the fused tracker has no estimate (0.9% of the gated rows, 2.5% of all rows).

    Nothing is trained or tuned.

  • The gate (G8): pass G5 against the reference IMM, and have a median range error wholly below the fused tracker's.

  • Pre-registration. The rules, the validation run and the decision record were all committed before the test data existed.

Result: G8 passes (seed 224, 40,000 scenarios)​

Hybrid againstRangeCourseSettled rangeSettled courseSettled speed
The IMM (limit 1 / 1.1)0.6890.8950.7240.8870.898
The fused tracker (limit 1)0.908 [0.893, 0.921]

The checks use 67,292 rows from the 20,161 test scenarios at σ = 1°. T2 converged on every row. Every 97.5% interval sits wholly within its limit. Five independent reviewers agreed that the verdict is right.

Why this is mostly a replay​

The panel was blunt about how much this pass adds:

  • The range is T2's, row for row. The check against the fused tracker is therefore T2 against the fused tracker. Phase 7 already showed that (fused/T2 1.095), and phase 8 showed no tuning closes it. Seed 224 replicates that result.
  • The velocity is the fused tracker's, which already passed G5 in phase 7.
  • The estimator to beat changed. In phases 8–9 it was T2; here it is the fused tracker, by the owner's choice before validation. T2 is still not beaten on range.
  • What is new is only that the pair, assembled per output, passes every G5 check at once.
  • About 1 point of each margin comes from the scoring convention. A row with no estimate counts as infinitely wrong, and the hybrid never lacks one. On rows where every estimator converged the ratios are 0.700 against the IMM and 0.918 against the fused tracker. Both still pass.

What it does not show​

  • No gain over T2 on range, since the range is T2's. T2 alone is also slightly better on overall course.
  • Classical filters still win on straight legs. The EKF is better on settled course (10.7° against 13.3°) and speed. Before any manoeuvre, batch least squares is best (7.3%, 2.2°).
  • It is not a track. The position comes from one estimator and the velocity from another. The position change over a minute disagrees with the stated velocity by a median 18 kt (8 kt for the fused tracker; 17 kt for T2 alone, whose position track is itself jumpy), and the implied bearing rate is the worst of the four estimators. It is a set of per-output estimates, not a state you could extrapolate.
  • No uncertainty is stated for the combined estimate.
  • Scope: one error model at one severity, on simulated data.

The arc, phases 7–10​

Under realistic bearing error:

  • the learned range estimate is the most robust;
  • feeding it into a filter costs some of that robustness;
  • tuning the fusion doesn't recover it;
  • modelling the bias inside the filter fails, because the bias can't be observed.

Reporting the learned range directly keeps its accuracy, at the cost of a coherent track.
