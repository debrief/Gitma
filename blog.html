Phase 11: retraining T2 on realistic bearing error does not improve its range

November 14, 2026 · 3 min read

Phases 7–10 showed that under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), the learned network T2 gives the most robust range estimate. T2 had been trained only on white noise. The obvious next question: does training it on the realistic error make it better still?

The test​

  • The model. T2's architecture and configuration, unchanged, trained from scratch on a fresh 100,000-scenario dataset generated entirely with realistic error. Training took about 16 hours on GitHub Actions and stopped early at epoch 48.
  • The gate (G8). The retrained T2 must:
    • have a median range error wholly below the frozen T2's;
    • pass G5 against the reference IMM.
  • The validation step first. The six point estimates on the shared validation split must all be within their limits before any test data are generated.

Result: no gain on range, so it stops at validation​

Retrained T2 againstRangeCourseSettled course
The frozen T21.021 [0.991, 1.052]0.9810.878
The IMM0.7450.8571.079
  • Range is unchanged. 12.4% against the frozen T2's 12.2%, and the interval also rules out a gain of more than 1%.
  • Settled course improves by about 12%, mostly on straight legs before any manoeuvre.
  • It still beats the IMM on overall range and course, and passes G5's settled limits.

The cost: worse on white noise​

On the same scenarios with white noise only, the retrained T2 is clearly worse:

Median range errorWhite noiseRealistic error
Retrained T210.3%12.4%
Frozen T28.0%12.2%
IMM9.1%16.7%

The retrained T2 looks more robust (it degrades 1.21 × against 1.52 ×), but only because it starts worse. On white noise it loses to the IMM on range.

Baselines (realistic error, all gated rows)​

  • Batch least squares: 17.1% and 46.0°. It is best before any manoeuvre (6.9%, 2.0°).
  • EKF: 16.1% and 29.7°. It is best on settled course (9.8°) and speed (1.91 kt).
  • Naive: 38.7% and 88.3°.
  • Fused tracker and hybrid: rebuilt on the retrained T2, they are unchanged (within 0.3 points in range).

What it means​

Seeing the error in training did not buy range accuracy. A plausible reading is that the frozen T2 is already near what the bearings allow under this error model: phase 9 found the drifting bias nearly unobservable, and a network cannot learn what the data do not contain.

Limits:

  • one training run, so the run-to-run spread is unknown;
  • one error model at one severity;
  • validation data only;
  • an architecture that was not re-tuned.

No test data were generated. Phase 10's verdict stands, and the owner decides what comes next.

Phase 10: T2's range with a filter's velocity passes G8, but adds little that was not known

November 12, 2026 · 4 min read

Phases 7–9 left one clear lesson. Under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), the learned network T2 gives the most robust range, and no change to the filters closes the gap. Phase 10 takes the direct route and reports T2's range alongside the fused tracker's course and speed.

The test​

  • The estimator:

    • range and position from T2;
    • course and speed from the frozen fused tracker;
    • T2's whole row wherever the fused tracker has no estimate (0.9% of the gated rows, 2.5% of all rows).

    Nothing is trained or tuned.

  • The gate (G8): pass G5 against the reference IMM, and have a median range error wholly below the fused tracker's.

  • Pre-registration. The rules, the validation run and the decision record were all committed before the test data existed.

Result: G8 passes (seed 224, 40,000 scenarios)​

Hybrid againstRangeCourseSettled rangeSettled courseSettled speed
The IMM (limit 1 / 1.1)0.6890.8950.7240.8870.898
The fused tracker (limit 1)0.908 [0.893, 0.921]

The checks use 67,292 rows from the 20,161 test scenarios at σ = 1°. T2 converged on every row. Every 97.5% interval sits wholly within its limit. Five independent reviewers agreed that the verdict is right.

Why this is mostly a replay​

The panel was blunt about how much this pass adds:

  • The range is T2's, row for row. The check against the fused tracker is therefore T2 against the fused tracker. Phase 7 already showed that (fused/T2 1.095), and phase 8 showed no tuning closes it. Seed 224 replicates that result.
  • The velocity is the fused tracker's, which already passed G5 in phase 7.
  • The estimator to beat changed. In phases 8–9 it was T2; here it is the fused tracker, by the owner's choice before validation. T2 is still not beaten on range.
  • What is new is only that the pair, assembled per output, passes every G5 check at once.
  • About 1 point of each margin comes from the scoring convention. A row with no estimate counts as infinitely wrong, and the hybrid never lacks one. On rows where every estimator converged the ratios are 0.700 against the IMM and 0.918 against the fused tracker. Both still pass.

What it does not show​

Median error, all G5 rowsRangeCourseSpeed
Hybrid12.0%26.6°2.58 kt
Fused tracker13.2%27.1°2.60 kt
T2 alone12.0%25.9°2.85 kt
IMM17.4%29.7°2.87 kt
EKF16.6%30.2°2.41 kt
Batch least squares18.0%46.6°2.50 kt
Naive40.2%89.9°3.99 kt
  • No gain over T2 on range, since the range is T2's. T2 alone is also slightly better on overall course.
  • Classical filters still win on straight legs. The EKF is better on settled course (10.7° against 13.3°) and speed. Before any manoeuvre, batch least squares is best (7.3%, 2.2°).
  • It is not a track. The position comes from one estimator and the velocity from another. The position change over a minute disagrees with the stated velocity by a median 18 kt (8 kt for the fused tracker; 17 kt for T2 alone, whose position track is itself jumpy), and the implied bearing rate is the worst of the four estimators. It is a set of per-output estimates, not a state you could extrapolate.
  • No uncertainty is stated for the combined estimate.
  • Scope: one error model at one severity, on simulated data.

The arc, phases 7–10​

Under realistic bearing error:

  • the learned range estimate is the most robust;
  • feeding it into a filter costs some of that robustness;
  • tuning the fusion doesn't recover it;
  • modelling the bias inside the filter fails, because the bias can't be observed.

Reporting the learned range directly keeps its accuracy, at the cost of a coherent track.

Phase 9: a bias-aware IMM cannot see the bias

November 12, 2026 · 2 min read

Under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), the learned range estimate T2 beat the classical IMM, and no fusion setting could close the gap (phase 8). The natural classical answer is to model the error: give the IMM a sixth state for the bearing bias and let it estimate the bias.

The test​

  • The filter: the reference IMM, plus a drifting bias added to every predicted bearing.
  • Tuning: the bias's size and time constant and the measurement noise scale, 18 settings in all, on validation data with realistic error.
  • The rule, fixed before any setting was scored: to go to a gate, a setting must beat T2 on range and still pass G5 against the plain IMM.
  • The forecast, recorded beforehand: this would probably fail. A quick check had shown that a 1° offset barely changes how well a straight-line track fits the bearings.

Result: it stops at validation​

Bias-state IMMvs plain IMM, rangevs T2, range
Best setting (0.25°, 10 min, r 8)0.9981.37
Larger bias (1°)1.05–1.121.44–1.53
  • At best it matches the plain IMM. It never improves on it.
  • It stays about 1.4 × T2's range error.
  • Allowing a larger bias makes it worse. The extra freedom is not pinned down by the bearings.

Why​

From bearings alone, a slowly drifting bias looks almost exactly like a slightly rotated target track. One or two ownship zigs do not separate the two, so the filter cannot learn the bias. T2 does better on range, plausibly because it learns range from the whole bearing history. That explanation was not tested.

Where this leaves things​

Phases 7–9 tell one story. Under this error model:

  • the learned range is the most robust estimate;
  • fusing it into a filter that assumes white noise loses some of that advantage;
  • modelling the bias inside the filter does not help, because the bias cannot be observed.

Two options remain for the owner:

  • report T2's range directly, as a hybrid;
  • close out the project.

Record: experiments/20261112-p9-research/; spec 021; SRD Annex C §C.16.

Phase 8: re-tuning the fusion cannot make it beat T2 on range under realistic error

November 10, 2026 · 3 min read

Phase 7 found a gap. Under realistic bearing error (correlated noise plus a drifting bias), T2 on its own had lower range error than the fused tracker that feeds T2's range into the IMM: 12.1% against 13.2%. The fusion's weighting had been tuned on clean data and then frozen, so the obvious question was whether re-tuning it closes the gap.

The test​

The owner chose to re-tune only the fusion's two settings, on validation data with realistic error:

  • k, how much to trust T2's range;
  • M, how often to feed it in.

The gate would require both of these:

  • beat T2 alone on median range error;
  • still pass G5 against the reference IMM.

The grid had 24 settings. The rule for choosing between them was written and committed before any of them was scored. A setting went to the gate only if its estimated margin on every check was positive.

Result: no setting qualifies​

SettingFused / IMM rangeFused / T2 range
Frozen (k 4, every 6 samples)0.7771.064
Best re-tune (k 3, every sample)0.7401.014
Heaviest weight on T2 (k 0.5, every sample)0.7491.027
  • Re-tuning helps against the IMM. Feeding T2's range at every sample cuts the fused tracker's range error further (0.74 × the IMM's), and cuts its shortfall against T2 from 6.4% to 1.4%.
  • It never beats T2. The best setting is still 1.4% worse on range. Trusting T2 more does not help either: course and velocity then fall apart (course up to 1.27 × the IMM's on settled rows), and range still trails T2.
  • So phase 8 stops at validation. No fresh test data were generated, and phase 7's verdict stands.

Why, plausibly​

Under the drifting bias, the IMM's range ends up about 7% short. Every bearing update pulls the shared track state back towards that biased range, so a single filter state cannot hold T2's less biased range, however the two are weighted. This explanation was not tested here.

What it means​

Within this design, fusing T2's range into a filter that assumes white noise costs some range accuracy under correlated error. The filter's state stays coherent, and it still beats the IMM clearly.

Two further changes might close the gap:

  • a filter that models the bias;
  • reporting T2's range directly, as the phase-4b hybrid did.

Either would be a new decision for the owner.

Record: experiments/20261110-p8-research/ (grid, selection, validation note); spec 020; SRD Annex C §C.15.

Phase 7: realistic bearing error — the frozen fused tracker still beats the frozen IMM, but T2 alone is more robust

November 8, 2026 · 3 min read

So far every tracker in this project has seen only white bearing noise. Real bearings also carry correlated error and a slowly drifting bias. Phase 7 asks one question: does the fused tracker's G5 advantage over the reference IMM survive that error, with nothing re-trained or re-tuned?

The setup​

The owner chose three things:

  • frozen trackers;
  • an error model made of a correlated term (sd 1.0°, 30 s) and a drifting bias (sd 0.5°, 20 min). This setting is double the one first proposed. At σ = 1° the total bearing error goes from 1.0° to 1.5°;
  • G5's rules, unchanged.

The generator adds the error from its own random stream, so each scenario can be regenerated without it. The two versions have identical geometry and identical white noise, which makes a paired clean comparison possible.

Result: G5 passes (seed 218, 40,000 scenarios)​

Validation on seed 217 had already shown much the same: 0.777 for range and 0.922 for course. Five independent reviewers agreed the verdict is right, and one reproduced it byte for byte.

What the pass does not show​

The absolute errors are large: the fused tracker's 90th-percentile course error is 106°. The panel narrowed the claim.

Median error, all G5 rowsRangeCourseSpeed
Fused tracker13.2%27°2.6 kt

  • T2 alone beats the fused tracker on range, by 1.10 × [1.08, 1.11] overall. Its error grows least under realistic error (1.47 × its clean value, against the IMM's 1.82 ×). The IMM's range ends up biased about 7% short under the drifting bias, while T2 stays nearly unbiased. The fusion setting, frozen from clean data, plausibly gives T2 too little weight; k was not re-swept. Fusing keeps one coherent track state, but under this error it is not the best range estimator.
  • On straight legs, classical methods still win. On settled rows the EKF beats the fused tracker on course (10.6° against 13.5°) and on speed, and batch least squares does too (8.1°, 1.8 kt). Before any manoeuvre, batch least squares is best of all: 7.3% range and 2.2° course.
  • The reference was frozen, not adapted. Neither filter models correlated or bias error. An IMM with an inflated measurement noise or a bias state might close the gap; this phase does not test that.
  • Every tracker is much worse. Median range error rises 1.5–1.8 × overall and about 2 × on settled rows.
  • Not measured. Filter consistency (NIS, NEES) and covariance health (non-PD steps) under correlated error.
  • Scope. The result covers one error model at one severity, σ = 1° rows and simulated data.

The one-line version​

Under one setting of realistic bearing error, the frozen fused tracker keeps its G5 advantage over the frozen IMM, which was not adapted to this error. The learned range (T2) is likely what makes it robust (a hypothesis), and on its own T2 is more robust still. The classical filters remain better on straight legs.

Phase 6 re-score: G7 passes, with coverage within tolerance on average but not calibrated

November 7, 2026 · 3 min read

G7 on seed 215 was inconclusive: the fused tracker's 50% range interval covered 48.3%, and its confidence interval straddled the 48% floor. The rules allow one re-score on a larger fresh seed. The owner chose to run it. Decision 34 fixed the details before the data existed:

  • What stayed the same: the tracker, the reference, the frozen models, the multipliers and the rules.
  • What changed: a fresh seed, 216, and a test split of 110,000 scenarios instead of 40,000. That is large enough to resolve a coverage of about 48.3% against the floor.
  • The forecast: about an even chance of passing.

Result: pass​

Coverage on seed 216 (184,667 G5 rows)50%90%
Range (log)48.6% [48.3, 48.9]89.9%
Course50.5%90.3%
Speed49.4%89.9%

All nine gated checks pass. The fused tracker's 90% intervals are narrower than the IMM's: 0.73 × for range, 0.94 × for course and 0.89 × for speed. This was the one re-score, so the verdict is final.

What the pass does and does not show​

Five independent reviewers agreed that the pass is correct. One recomputed every value with code of its own, and another re-ran the gate. All five also agreed on how far the claim goes.

  • Range intervals run about 1.5 points short. At 50%, the two seeds together give about 48.5% [48.3, 48.8]. That is inside the ±2-point tolerance but not centred on 50%. The pass rests on the tolerance. The reference IMM, fitted on the same validation data, lands on 50%. So reusing validation data does not explain the fused tracker's shortfall.

  • The intervals cover within tolerance only on the pooled mix of rows. Range at 50% covers:

    RowsCoverage
    Settled, before any manoeuvre (quiet legs)67%
    Settled, after a manoeuvre39%
    At zig + 5 min61%
    At zig + 20 min40%

    Seed 215 showed the same pattern within a point.

  • Course and speed intervals are often uninformative.

    • 17% of course 90% intervals span the whole circle, and the median course 90% interval is 188° wide. The course width ratio of 0.94 therefore compares two mostly vague intervals.
    • 27% of speed 90% intervals reach below zero.

  • This was a second look. Over both seeds, the chance of a false pass is about 5% at most, not 2.5%. A smaller re-score would probably have been inconclusive again.

  • The pass judges these frozen multipliers. The 0.3-point margin is smaller than the multipliers' own sampling error. So the pass does not show that the recalibration procedure works in general.

Phase 6 in one line​

On average over the G5 rows, the fused tracker's recalibrated intervals cover within the tolerance, with range about 1.5 points short, and they are tighter than the IMM's. Within a regime they do not: the intervals are too wide on quiet legs and too narrow after a manoeuvre, and its course intervals are often vacuous. Conditional calibration, for example a multiplier that depends on time since the last manoeuvre, would be a new design. The owner decides on the follow-on.

Phase 6: the fused tracker's uncertainty, recalibrated, is inconclusive at G7

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

C.6's second follow-on asks whether the tracker that passed G5 can say how sure it is. The fused tracker is the frozen IMM with T2's range fused in.

The owner chose two things:

  • Calibrate the fused tracker's stated uncertainty, without training.
  • The test: calibrated and sharper. Its 50% and 90% intervals for range, course and speed must cover within ±2 points of nominal, and be narrower than the reference IMM's after the same recalibration.

What was done​

  • Stated sds. Both trackers now write them, from their covariance by the delta method. They are badly miscalibrated as stated: typical errors are a fraction of the stated sd, with heavy tails.
  • Recalibration, post hoc. One pair of multipliers per output, fitted on validation, scales the sd into 50% and 90% intervals. A two-fold check on validation passed, and decision 33 froze the multipliers before seed 215 was generated for scoring.

Result: inconclusive​

Coverage on seed 215 (G5 rows)50%90%
Range (log)48.3% [47.8, 48.8]89.8%
Course50.5%90.2%
Speed49.3%89.6%

The fused tracker's intervals are narrower than the IMM's: 0.73 × for range, 0.95 × for course and 0.89 × for speed. Eight of the nine checks pass. Range at 50% falls just short: its interval straddles the 48% floor.

What the panel added​

Five independent reviewers agreed the verdict is right, and reproduced it byte for byte. They also narrowed what can be claimed:

  • The intervals are calibrated only on the pooled mix of rows. On quiet straight legs before any manoeuvre they are far too wide: range 50% covers 67%. After a manoeuvre they are too narrow: 38%. One multiplier cannot fix that shape.
  • Course and speed intervals are often uninformative. 17% of course 90% intervals span the whole circle, and 27% of speed 90% intervals reach below zero.
  • The shortfall looks systematic, not noise. The likely cause is that T2's training epoch, the fusion settings and the multipliers were all chosen on the same validation split.
  • The rules were the agent's. The owner did not get to review them before the decision record.

What next​

The rules allow one re-score on a larger fresh seed. It would probably land near 48.3% again, so a pass would mean the range coverage just reaches the floor, not that the tracker is well calibrated. The owner decides whether to run it.

Record: experiments/20261106-p6u-g7/; spec 018.

Phase 5: frozen models do not beat a CUSUM at detecting manoeuvres

November 5, 2026 · 3 min read

G5 passed in phases 4b and 4c. SRD Annex C §C.6 therefore lists incremental follow-ons, and the first is manoeuvre detection, judged against a CUSUM on the reference tracker's innovations.

The owner chose two things for this attempt:

  • No training. The detector is built from models that already exist: the frozen recurrent tracker T2, and the reference IMM, which now uses decision 32's guarded initialisation.
  • The test. Detection delay, with every detector's threshold set for the same false-alarm rate: one alarm per 2 h of quiet tracking.

What was compared​

  • The baseline. A CUSUM on the IMM's innovations, tuned on validation from 12 declared settings to favour it.
    • Clipping. The innovations are heavy-tailed: a few huge values otherwise hold an alarm for hours. So both the variance kind and the mean-shift kind are clipped.
    • The winner. The two-sided mean-shift CUSUM, since a turn biases the bearings to one side.
  • The learned detectors. Two candidates, declared in advance:
    • T2's course disagreement with the IMM;
    • T2's own course change over the last minute.

Result: it stopped at validation​

At 0.5 false alarms per hour, on 7,479 course changes in seed-211 validation:

Detected within 5 min
CUSUM (mean shift)28.2%
T2's course change over 1 min19.2%
T2 against the IMM5.9%
  • The learned detector is not carried. It is 9 points behind, with interval [−10.2, −7.7] pp, so it was not taken to a test split, and fresh seed 215 was never generated.
  • The delay check could not be judged. Every detector misses more than half the course changes at this rate, so the median delays are infinite. That rule was the agent's, written before the data; any later attempt should declare a delay statistic defined at its operating point.

Reported, not used: a crossover​

The same detectors were also calibrated at looser rates:

False alarms per hour0.5125
CUSUM28.2%38.0%47.8%62.6%
T2's course change19.2%39.0%58.1%71.7%

From about 1 false alarm per hour, T2's course change catches more manoeuvres in time, though about 20–40 s later among those both catch. Moving the operating point after seeing this would choose the rule from the result, so it stays a reported observation.

What next​

The owner decides. Annex C's other follow-ons remain:

  • calibration of the learned uncertainty;
  • realistic bearing error.
